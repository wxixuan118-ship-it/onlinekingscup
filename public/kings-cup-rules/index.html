Kings Cup Rules: Every Card Explained

Need a rule right now? Scan the card chart, learn how to play in five steps, then check the fourth King, the 5-and-6 argument, and how to play it without alcohol.

Kings Cup Online editors · Updated September 14, 2026

Kings Cup rules in one sentence: Draw one card clockwise and do what its rank says — 2 is You, 3 is Me, 7 is Heaven — while the first three Kings pour into the cup in the middle and whoever draws the fourth King drinks it.

What Is Kings Cup?

Kings Cup is a drinking card game for 3–10 players built around one shuffled 52-card deck spread face down in a ring around a cup. There is no scoring, no teams and no winner. Every rank in the deck is mapped to an action — a reaction round, a word game, a prompt, or a role that lasts several turns — so the deck itself is the rulebook. Once the group agrees on what each card means, the game runs itself.

The same game travels under several names. Ring of Fire is the usual name in the UK, Ireland and Australia, Circle of Death is common in the US college scene, and King’s Game or Waterfall turn up regionally. They all describe the same loop; only a few card actions differ, and those come from house rules rather than from the name a group uses — Wikipedia catalogues the naming under King game.

It is normally played as a drinking game — how to play the Kings Cup drinking game below covers setup, turns and the four Kings — with the fourth King finishing the center cup. Nothing in the structure requires alcohol though, which is why the points-based and family versions play just as well — see playing without alcohol below.

How to Play the Kings Cup Drinking Game

Here is how to play Kings Cup from the first draw to the fourth King — the Kings Cup drinking game takes about two minutes to learn. You need one standard 52-card deck (Jokers optional), three to ten players, a large cup for the middle of the table and a drink for each player — or a challenge bowl and soft drinks for the non-drinking version.

Set up the table

Seat everyone around a stable surface (four to eight players plays best), put the King’s Cup in the center, and spread the shuffled deck face down in an unbroken ring around it. Before the first draw, agree on the card meanings, the rules for 5, 6 and Jack, how long an answer may take, and whether the game ends on the fourth King or after the whole deck. The Kings Cup setup guide has the full checklist, remote setup and player-count advice.

Fast setup: No physical deck? Open Kings Cup Online. The virtual deck shuffles, removes drawn cards and shows the rule after every draw.

How a turn works

  1. Pick a starting player. Play moves clockwise.
  2. On your turn, draw one card from the ring and read its rule aloud.
  3. Carry the rule out before the next player draws. Nominate cards (2 You, 3 Me) send a drink to one person; reaction cards (4 Floor, 7 Heaven) and word games (9 Rhyme, 10 Categories) make the slowest player or the first to slip up drink; Ace is Waterfall, where everyone drinks together.
  4. Role cards stay live after the turn ends: Thumb Master lasts until another 5 is drawn, Mate pairs two players until another 8 replaces the partnership, Question Master lasts until another Queen, and Make a Rule, when used for Jack, lasts until the group removes or replaces it.
  5. Track the Kings. The first three players to draw a King each pour some of their drink into the King’s Cup; whoever draws the fourth King drinks it, and the game ends.

A typical game runs 20–45 minutes. If the group would rather keep going, finish the King’s Cup on the fourth King and keep drawing until the deck is empty — but pick the ending before you start so the fourth King is never disputed. You do not need to memorize all thirteen cards for a first game: learn Ace, 2, 3 and the three face cards, then look the rest up in the chart above as they come.

Drink pacing. Keep sips small, alternate with water, put out food, and let anyone swap in a soft drink for any card. Never enforce a rule that pressures someone to keep up, and never play with anyone under the legal drinking age.

Every Card Rule with a Play Example

The turn structure is the same everywhere, even though groups assign different actions to a few ranks. These examples show exactly how each rule plays out at the table.

Ace — Waterfall

Everyone starts drinking at the same time. The player who drew the Ace can stop whenever they like; each following player may stop only after the person immediately before them stops, so the last player drinks the longest. Nobody has to finish a drink.

Example

Maya draws an Ace and the whole table starts. Maya puts her cup down, which frees Leo, then Nina, and finally Sam, who has been drinking the entire time.

2 — You

The drawer points at any player and that player drinks. Some groups let the drawer split two drinks between two people instead of giving both to one.

Example

Leo draws a 2 and picks Sam. Sam drinks.

3 — Me

The person who drew the card drinks. The rhyme “three is me” makes it the easiest rule for new players to remember.

Example

Nina turns over a 3, so Nina drinks. Nobody else is involved.

4 — Floor

Everyone at the table reaches down and touches the floor. The last person to get there drinks. Keep the area clear so nobody dives off a chair.

Example

Sam draws a 4 and slaps the floor. Three players follow instantly, but Leo reacts last, so Leo drinks.

5 — Thumb Master

The drawer becomes Thumb Master until the next 5 is drawn. At any point in the game, the Thumb Master quietly rests a thumb on the table’s edge; everyone else has to copy, and the last one to notice drinks. Older lists play 5 as Guys, where every man at the table drinks — see variations.

Example

Maya draws a 5. Two turns later she drops her thumb mid-conversation. Nina notices last and drinks.

6 — Challenge

The drawer starts the short challenge the group agreed on before play — a three-second category, an accent, a staring contest — and whoever fails it drinks. Six is the most variable card in the deck, so pin it down before the first draw. The traditional rhyming version is Chicks, where every woman at the table drinks.

Example

Leo draws a 6. The agreed challenge is naming an animal within three seconds. Leo fires it at Sam, who says “otter” in time, so nobody drinks.

7 — Heaven

Everyone points a hand at the sky the moment the card is flipped. The last person to raise a hand drinks.

Example

Nina draws a 7 and points up. Everyone follows except Maya, who is still looking at the table, so Maya drinks.

8 — Mate

The drawer picks a mate. From then on, whenever one of them drinks, the other drinks too. The pairing usually lasts the rest of the game, though some groups end it when the next 8 appears.

Example

Sam draws an 8 and picks Leo. On the next turn Leo draws a 3, so Leo drinks — and so does Sam.

9 — Rhyme

The drawer says a word. Moving clockwise, each player has to say a word that rhymes with it. The first player to repeat a word, miss the rhyme or run out of time drinks.

Example

Maya starts with “light.” The table answers “night,” “bright” and “kite.” Sam repeats “night,” so Sam drinks.

10 — Categories

The drawer names a category. Players go around naming things in it until someone repeats an answer, gives an invalid one or stalls. That player drinks.

Example

Leo picks “pizza toppings.” The table says mushroom, pepper, olive, onion. Nina says olive again, so Nina drinks.

Jack — Never Have I Ever

The drawer says something they have never done, and everyone who has done it drinks. Keep it light — nobody should have to reveal anything they would rather not, and any player can pass. Many groups play Jack as Make a Rule instead, where the drawer invents a rule that lasts until it is replaced.

Example

Nina says, “Never have I ever missed a flight.” Maya and Sam have, so they both drink.

Queen — Question Master

The drawer becomes Question Master until the next Queen. Anyone who answers one of their questions drinks — the only escape is to reply with a question of your own. Genuine safety questions and rule clarifications should never count as traps.

Example

Sam is Question Master and casually asks Leo what time it is. Leo says “half nine” before remembering, so Leo drinks.

King — Kings Cup

The first three players to draw a King each pour some of their drink into the King’s Cup in the middle of the table. Whoever draws the fourth King drinks the whole thing, and that ends the game. Never build an unsafe mixture, and never pressure anyone to finish it — a challenge bowl or a final dare keeps the same four-King suspense.

Example

Three Kings are already gone when Maya flips the last one. She drinks the King’s Cup and the game is over.

How the Ring of Fire Works

Ring of Fire is the same game named after the circle of face-down cards rather than the cup, which is why groups using that name treat the ring itself as part of the rules (the Kings Cup vs Ring of Fire guide covers the naming in full). Lay the cards face down in a single overlapping circle around the cup with no gaps — too tight and clean draws are impossible, too loose and the circle breaks constantly. With fewer than five players, spread a wider ring so nobody has to lean across the table.

Breaking the ring

Breaking the ring means opening a visible gap or dragging a neighbouring card out of place while drawing. This is the rule that varies most between tables, so settle it out loud before the first draw:

House ruleWhat a break costsBest for
Strict ringWhoever broke it drinks immediately, then re-spreads the circleExperienced groups who want the extra tension
StandardForfeit only if a card is pushed fully clear of the ringMost groups — the usual default
RelaxedNo forfeit; close the gap and carry onMixed-experience tables and crowded surfaces
PointsOne penalty point instead of a drinkNon-drinking and family games

The circle thins as cards are drawn. Once it stops looking like a ring, pause and re-spread the remaining cards into a smaller circle rather than letting them drift into piles. Re-spreading is never a forfeit, and any player can call for it.

Agree the ring rule first. The broken-ring penalty causes more mid-game arguments than any card in the deck. Pick a row from the table above before anyone draws.

The Rules Players Ask About Most

How does Waterfall work?

When an Ace is drawn, everyone starts drinking at the same time. The player who drew the Ace stops whenever they want. Moving clockwise, each player may stop only after the person before them has stopped, so the last player drinks the longest. Nobody has to finish a drink.

What are 5 and 6 in Kings Cup?

The traditional rhyming rules are 5 is Guys and 6 is Chicks — every man, or every woman, at the table drinks. Many groups now play 5 as Thumb Master and 6 as a group Challenge instead, because those work at any table. Neither set is official; pick one before the first card is drawn.

How does Question Master work?

The Queen drawer becomes Question Master until the next Queen. Anyone who answers one of that player’s questions drinks. The usual counter is to reply with another question. Safety questions and genuine requests to clarify a rule should not count as traps.

What happens when a King is drawn?

The first, second and third players to draw a King each pour some of their drink into the King’s Cup in the middle of the table. Whoever draws the fourth King drinks the whole cup and the game ends. Never mix spirits into an unsafe combination and never pressure anyone to finish it.

Kings Cup Variations

Kings Cup variations come in two kinds: swapping what a single card does, and changing the format of the whole game. Either way, keep the draw-and-follow loop and settle the changes before the first card is drawn.

Card swaps

No single card list is universal. The most common swaps are Jack as Make a Rule instead of Never Have I Ever, 5 as Guys and 6 as Chicks instead of Thumb Master and Challenge, Ace as Snake Eyes (nobody may make eye contact with the drawer) instead of Waterfall, and 2 as Two for You, where the drawer splits two drinks between two people. Some tables add Jokers as a Wild Rule or Reverse Direction card, and many add a penalty for breaking the ring. See alternative card rules for a rank-by-rank list and fair house rules for timing, passes and disputes.

Game formats

  • Short game: use half a deck or a 20-minute timer; the last King before time runs out triggers the finale.
  • Large group: with ten or more players, play in teams and cap word games at five seconds so turns keep moving.
  • Two players or couples: replace the group-dependent cards (Floor, Heaven, Mate) with shared prompts — see Kings Cup for two.
  • Remote: one host draws from the online deck on a video call and pauses after each card; pick camera-friendly actions.
  • Points: one penalty point per lost challenge instead of a drink; lowest total wins when the deck runs out.
  • Challenge bowl: each King adds a written dare instead of a drink, and the fourth King draws one for the group.
  • Custom deck: each player replaces one card rule before play, or build a full set with the rule generator. Write it down so it stays consistent.

Change only a few elements at once — a short remote game or a points-based family game stays easy to follow, while stacking several custom sets slows every turn. For all ten formats and how to choose between them, see the full Kings Cup variations guide.

Can Kings Cup Be Played Without Alcohol?

Yes. The game works because of the card prompts and reaction rounds, not the alcohol. Replace every drink with one penalty point, a sip of water or soda, a trivia question, or a short dare. Swap the center cup for a challenge bowl and give every player a pass they never have to explain. The non-drinking Kings Cup guide has a complete substitution system.

Play responsibly. Know your limit, never pressure anyone to drink, and do not play this game with anyone under the legal drinking age.

Kings Cup Rules FAQ

What is the Kings Cup drinking game?

Kings Cup is a drinking card game for 3–10 players. Cards are spread face down in a ring around a center cup, players draw one card each clockwise, and every rank triggers an action: someone drinks, a reaction round, a word game or a role that lasts several turns. The first three Kings pour into the center cup and the fourth King drinks it. Every drinking action can be swapped for a point, a sip of water or a dare, so the same rules work as a non-drinking game.

How do you play Kings Cup?

Spread a shuffled 52-card deck face down in a ring around a center cup. Players take turns clockwise, drawing one card and doing what its rank says: Ace is Waterfall, 2 is You, 3 is Me, 4 is Floor, 5 is Thumb Master, 6 is Challenge, 7 is Heaven, 8 is Mate, 9 is Rhyme, 10 is Categories, Jack is Never Have I Ever, Queen is Question Master and King is Kings Cup. The first three Kings pour into the center cup, and whoever draws the fourth King drinks it, which ends the game. The how to play section above walks a first game through in order.

What does each card mean in Kings Cup?

Ace is Waterfall, 2 is You, 3 is Me, 4 is Floor, 5 is Thumb Master, 6 is Challenge, 7 is Heaven, 8 is Mate, 9 is Rhyme, 10 is Categories, Jack is Never Have I Ever, Queen is Question Master and King is Kings Cup. House rules vary most on 5, 6 and Jack, so agree on those before the first draw — the card rules chart above is the full list.

What happens if you break the ring in Ring of Fire?

It depends on the house rule you picked. A strict table makes whoever broke the circle drink immediately and re-spread it; the common default is a forfeit only when a card is pushed fully clear of the ring; relaxed tables just close the gap and carry on. Decide which one you are using before the first draw — see how the ring works.

How many players do you need for Kings Cup?

Three to ten players works well. Four to eight offers the best balance of variety and turn speed, while the two-player version replaces group-dependent rules.

Do you need Jokers to play Kings Cup?

No. A standard 52-card deck is enough. If Jokers are included, define their meaning before the game; common choices are Wild Rule, Reverse Direction or Remove a Rule.

Does the game end on the fourth King?

Usually, but not always. The fourth King drinks the center cup and most groups stop there. Some groups finish the King’s Cup and keep drawing until every card is gone.

Is Kings Cup the same as Ring of Fire?

Yes. Same game, same core loop. “Ring of Fire” is the name used mostly outside North America, and “Circle of Death” is another alias. A few card actions, the broken-circle penalty and the fourth-King finale change by house rules, not by which name you use. See Kings Cup vs Ring of Fire for a full comparison of the two names, and how the ring works above for the broken-ring penalty.

Can you play Kings Cup without alcohol?

Yes. Replace every drink with one penalty point, a sip of water or soda, a trivia question or a short dare, and swap the center cup for a challenge bowl. The card prompts and reaction rounds are what make the game work — see the non-drinking Kings Cup rules.
